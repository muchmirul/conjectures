Turn the stack, and open the gaps

The same stack as chapter 0's page: one ball and the twelve that touch it. Here you can turn it with the two dials, and pull the balls apart to see what that costs.

Try this. Leave the separation at 1.00 and turn the stack right round. Twelve balls touch the middle one from every angle, and the gaps never close: even packed as tightly as possible, this arrangement fills only 74.05 percent of space. Then type a smaller number into the percentage box and watch the balls drift apart to match it.

The separation is measured in ball diameters, so 1.00 means the balls touch and nothing can be pushed closer. Above that, every centre moves apart by the same factor, and the share of space filled falls with the cube of it. Pulling the centres a quarter further apart takes the tightest stack from 74.05 percent down to 37.91, which is very nearly half thrown away for a gap you can barely see. The percentage box works the other way round, solving for the separation that would give the figure you type.

Two arrangements are drawn. The greengrocer's stack puts twelve balls against the middle one and fills 74.05 percent of space; plain rows and columns put six against it and fill 52.36 percent. Kepler guessed in 1611 that nothing beats the first, and Hales proved it in 1998.

What the percentage describes. The thirteen or twenty-seven balls on the canvas are a fragment. The figure is the share of space filled by the endless arrangement they are cut from, not by the handful you can see, which would depend on where you drew the boundary. Both figures are computed on this page from the ball size and the spacing, and both agree with the values the article quotes.
